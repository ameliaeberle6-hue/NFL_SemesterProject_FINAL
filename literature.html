Social Media & Sports Organizations

NFL teams have really started to lean into Instagram as a way to connect with their fans in a more real, everyday way. When you actually look at what works, it’s not the super polished, overly promotional posts; it’s the behind-the-scenes moments, the locker room clips, the player personalities, and the little things fans wouldn’t normally get to see. That kind of content just feels more real, and people respond to it way more. At the end of the day, it shows that fans don’t just care about wins and stats—they care about feeling connected. Personality and access matter just as much as performance, if not more.

Athletic Performance & Fan Engagement

Several studies have examined this direct relationship between on-field success and fan behavior that we speak of. It has been found that winning percentage is positively connected to game attendance, merchandise sales, and overall media exposure. However, their findings also showed that this effect has variables. For example, teams that are consistently good do not see massive increases in engagement over time.

Celebrity Culture & Sports Crossover

An important aspect of sports social media growth that has not been talked about enough is the role of celebrity culture and media crossover. We see lots of correlation between the popularity of outside celebrities (not football players) and the engagement levels of sports teams' social media accounts if fans find them in a relationship or a fan of a team.

Instagram as a Research Subject

Instagram launched in 2010 and was acquired by Facebook in 2012. It has since become a main platform for sports content. Visual platforms such as this are largely well-suited to sports content because they capture the aesthetic and emotional aspects of athletic performance in ways that a text-only method simply cannot.

What We Still Don't Know

There is a growing body of research on this matter, but several questions remain. Most studies focus on short time windows, such as a single season or a few years. This makes it difficult to identify long-term trends. Few studies have truly tried to seperate the specific contribution of unique external events that go "viral" within our culture (celebrity news, viral moments, controversies, drama, etc) from performance-based growth. Our project contributes to addressing both of these gaps by examining a thirteen-year dataset and intentionally flagging known cultural events as variables in our analysis.
